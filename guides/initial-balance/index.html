The initial balance (IB), for beginners

The first hour's high and low, range extensions, and how traders use the IB as a map for the rest of the day.

6 min read

The initial balance, or IB, is the price range of the first hour of the main trading session. It comes from market-profile trading and is one of the simplest ways to get a map of the day.

How to mark it

  1. Pick the session. For US index futures this is usually 9:30 to 10:30 ET.
  2. When the hour ends, mark the IB high and IB low.
  3. The distance between them is the IB range.

Range extension

When price trades beyond the IB high or low after the first hour, that is a range extension. Some traders also draw lines at 1× or 2× the IB range beyond the high and low, as reference points for how far the day has stretched. They are reference points, not targets.

Narrow and wide IBs

Traders compare today's IB with recent days:

  • Narrow IB: the first hour was quiet. Many traders watch these days for an extension later, because little of a normal day's range has been used.
  • Wide IB: the first hour already moved a lot. The stop for a breakout is wider, and a lot of the day's usual range may be spent.

Neither one tells you direction. They only describe how much room the day has used.

Three ways a day can treat the IB

Some days never leave the IB. Some extend in one direction and keep going. Others break one side, fail and run to the other. Seeing these three shapes makes it easier to read a chart without forcing a trade.

IB and ORB, side by side

Opening range (ORB)Initial balance (IB)
WindowFirst 5 to 30 minFirst 60 min
Range sizeSmallerLarger
Stop to the other sideTighterWider
Trades per dayEarlier, more oftenLater, fewer

Both mark early levels and watch for price to leave them. The IB simply waits longer, so its levels hold more of the session's early trading.

The IB is a way to read the day, not a signal. Any rule built on it needs to be written down, tested after costs and forward tested. The desk's IB research is in Research.
