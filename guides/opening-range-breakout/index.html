Opening range breakout (ORB), for beginners

What the opening range is, what counts as a breakout, where the risk sits and why many breakouts fail.

6 min read

The first minutes after a market opens are often the busiest of the session. An opening range breakout, or ORB, uses that burst of activity as a map: mark where price traded early, then watch what happens when it leaves that area.

The opening range

The opening range is simply the highest and lowest price of the first few minutes after a session opens. Common choices are the first 5, 15 or 30 minutes. For US index futures, many traders use the New York stock market open at 9:30 ET.

Once that time is up, the two levels are fixed for the day. Everything after is measured against them.

What counts as a breakout

A breakout is price moving outside the range. Traders define it in one of two ways, and the choice matters:

  • Touch: price trades one tick beyond the high or low. Earlier, but it catches more false moves.
  • Close: a candle closes beyond the level. Slower, but it filters some quick spikes.

A break above the high is read as buyers in control for now. A break below the low is the mirror image.

Where the risk sits

The range also gives a natural place for a stop. Two common references are the other side of the range or its midpoint.

That makes the range height your risk per contract. In the chart, the range is 40 points. On Micro Nasdaq (MNQ, $2 a point) a stop at the other side costs $80 per contract before costs. On the full contract (NQ, $20 a point) the same stop costs $800. A wide opening range means a wide stop, so size goes down, not up.

Most of the work is in the false breaks

Price often pokes out of the range and comes straight back. No rule removes this. What separates one ORB plan from another is how it handles these moments: where the stop sits, whether it trades a second break, and when it stops for the day.

Decide these before you test

  1. Market and session. Gold, Nasdaq and the Dow behave differently, and so do the Asia, London and New York opens.
  2. Range length. 5, 15 or 30 minutes.
  3. Entry trigger. Touch or close, and which side or both.
  4. Stop. Other side, midpoint or a fixed number of points.
  5. Exit. A target, a trailing stop or a time of day.
  6. Cut-off. The latest time a new trade is allowed.

ORB is a framework, not an edge. Whether any version works depends on the market, the exact rules and costs, and it changes over time. Write the rules down, test them after costs, and forward test before risking money. The desk's own ORB research is in Research.
