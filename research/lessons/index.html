Lessons from the failures.

What months of tests with no validated edge taught. Each one changed how the next test was run.

01

In-sample optimisation fools you

The 14 best opening range and Initial Balance picks made +$89,458 together on the year they were chosen from, with a $1,549 drawdown and a 75.5% win rate. On four earlier years they lost −$31,794 with a $34,196 drawdown. Search hard enough on one year and something will always look great on that year. See the evidence.

02

Run the random-direction null first

Rerun the whole search with random directions. If your best pick does not beat 95% of the random picks, it was selected by luck. This check caught every over-fitted strategy on the desk; simple replication checks caught none of them. See the evidence.

03

The limit fill-bar artifact

A backtest that fills a limit order and hits the target on the same bar is counting a trade that may never have happened. Early high-win-rate, small-target results came from this. Now the target only counts from the bar after the fill, and any high win rate with a small target is treated as a bug until it is replicated.

04

Drawdown caps must allow 'no pick'

A risk limit is a hard filter. When nothing fits under the $2,000 drawdown cap, the answer is no strategy, not the closest one. A soft cap once let a pick through with a $3,763 drawdown.

05

Costs exceed gross edge on most retail setups

Many popular setups make between $0.10 and $2 per trade before costs. A micro contract round trip costs $2.20 to $3.90 here. Compare average gross profit per trade with the round-trip cost before anything else, and flag anything under about twice the cost. See the evidence.

06

Effects decay

The published first-half-hour to last-half-hour momentum effect measured +0.15 in 2021, +0.07 in 2022, about zero in 2023 and negative since 2024. A paper's full-period average can hide an effect that has already gone. See the evidence.

07

A high win rate is not an edge

One Initial Balance pick kept a 76% win rate on unseen years and still lost $2,733. Small targets win often; a few full stops take it back. Never rank on win rate. See the evidence.

08

Check both directions before costs

If the real side and the flipped side both lose before costs, no filter, exit or sizing rule will rescue the idea. Key-level retests failed this on 36 of 36 groups. See the evidence.

09

Use placebos for levels and confluence

Fake levels, fake strikes and shifted zones did as well as the real ones for volume profile and options walls. Ablations (removing one part at a time) are not enough. See the evidence.

10

Sizing scales risk, not edge

Fixed size, percent risk, equity steps, anti-martingale and Kelly-style rules all left the net per contract about the same. Streak-based rules were worse than fixed size, and percent-risk sizing loaded up on the worst, narrow-stop trades.

11

Watch concentration

When the best 10 days carry most of the profit, the result describes those days. The closest lead on this desk has 98% of its profit in its top 10 days. See the evidence.

12

Low-frequency ideas run out of power

An idea that trades about once a week cannot be confirmed or rejected on a few years of data. Calculate the power before the test, and close the idea honestly as 'insufficient evidence' when it is too low.

13

Combining weak strategies doesn't create an edge

Diversification smooths noise but cannot turn many zero-edge strategies into one positive edge. Fourteen small losers made one large loser. See the evidence.

14

Fixed-clock rules change with the seasons

Rules on fixed GMT+3 times shift by an hour against New York when US daylight saving changes. The noise-area lead made about $23 per trade in summer and about $2 in winter.

All dollar figures are hypothetical backtest results for one contract, after exchange and broker fees and one tick of slippage on each side of every market or stop order.
