This backtest made about thirty one thousand dollars in a year of gold.

Then I changed one thing. Every fill got one tick worse. The smallest price step gold can move.

The same trades, on the same days, ended about eighty five thousand dollars down.

One tick turned a nice-looking test into a heavy loss. Nothing about the entries or exits changed, only the price of getting in and out.

Today I want to show you how that happens, which kinds of strategies it breaks, and the fill tricks that make backtests look better than real trading.

The rule is a simple public sample, not one of my private strategies.

If a thirty minute gold candle closes above the previous candle's high, buy at the next open. Sell at the close of that same candle.

If it closes below the previous low, do the opposite.

One full gold contract, market orders, four dollars and fifty cents commission for each round trip.

It trades a lot, and every trade is short. Keep that in mind, because that is the whole story.

I fixed this rule, and every other rule in this video, before running a single number.

Here is one morning, the sixth of October.

The rule took eight trades between half past six and ten o'clock.

Some won close to nine hundred dollars, others lost about six hundred, and the last one lost about eleven hundred.

On that day alone it took twenty seven trades.

Each trade only lasts thirty minutes. That makes each one small, and it makes the fees add up very fast.

And every one of those results is before slippage. Hold on to that.

Over the full year the rule took five thousand eight hundred and forty three trades.

Before any costs, it made about fifty eight thousand dollars.

Commission took about twenty six thousand.

That left about thirty one thousand six hundred, or about five dollars and forty cents per trade.

On a chart, that line climbs. It looks like a result. But look at that per-trade number again. Five dollars and forty cents on a contract worth hundreds of thousands.

That is the kind of number that hides inside a nice looking equity curve.

Slippage is the difference between the price you see and the price you get.

On a market order, you usually pay a little more to buy and receive a little less to sell.

In gold, one tick is ten cents, which is ten dollars on one contract.

Each trade has two fills, one in and one out. So one tick per fill costs twenty dollars a trade.

Over eleven thousand six hundred and eighty six fills, that is about one hundred and seventeen thousand dollars.

Thirty one thousand minus one hundred and seventeen thousand. The year ends about eighty five thousand dollars down.

The trades did not change. Not one entry or exit moved by more than a single tick.

How much slippage could this rule survive?

With a quarter of a tick per fill, it still made about forty cents a trade.

With half a tick, it lost about four dollars and sixty cents.

The break-even point was about a quarter of a tick per fill.

Real orders do not fill in quarter ticks. Either you get your price or you do not. So this rule had no room at all.

If you ever see a fast rule with a tiny average per trade, run this check before anything else.

So I put four public sample rules on the same ladder.

Two are moving average crosses, one slow and one faster. Two are one-candle trades.

The slow cross held up even at three ticks per fill. Its break-even was about forty six ticks.

The faster cross could carry about eleven ticks.

The one-candle rule from the start could carry about a quarter of one tick. And the other one-candle rule lost money before slippage was even added.

Same market, same year, same fees. The only thing that changed was how long each trade lasted.

A test that only reports the zero tick column is hiding the most important number.

Here is the reason.

The slow cross caught about ninety two ticks per trade before costs. The one-candle rule caught about one tick.

The cost does not shrink when the trade gets shorter. Commission and slippage are about the same on a two day trade as on a thirty minute one.

So a rule that holds for minutes is fighting a fixed bill with a tiny move.

That is why the fastest rules are the most fragile. Not because they are wrong, but because there is almost nothing left after the bill.

None of this makes the slow crosses good strategies. They are just less sensitive to this one cost.

This is not only a gold problem.

On Nasdaq futures, a one-candle RSI rule made about thirty six thousand dollars before slippage.

With one tick per fill, about two and a half thousand. With two ticks, about thirty one thousand down.

A Nasdaq tick is worth five dollars, half of gold's, so the rule lasted a little longer.

But the shape is the same. A rule that trades often with tiny moves lives or dies on the fill.

And the slow Nasdaq cross, like the slow gold one, could carry about forty one ticks per fill.

There is a second warning in the same test.

Before slippage, the rule was up in seven months out of fourteen. With one tick, five.

The first half of the year made about eighty five thousand dollars. The second half lost about fifty three thousand, before slippage.

Most of the gain came from January and March, when gold moved violently.

So the clean line I showed you at the start was mostly two wild months, plus a fill assumption that real trading would not give.

Those splits were found after looking, so treat them as warnings, not as findings.

You might say: just use limit orders. A limit order never fills at a worse price.

That is true. But a limit order has its own problem. It does not always fill.

And the trades it misses are not random ones.

So I rebuilt the same rule with a limit order at the signal close, held to the next close, with no slippage at all.

The question was simple. Does avoiding slippage save the rule?

If every touch of the limit counts as a fill, five thousand seven hundred and fifty two trades filled. Ninety one did not.

The filled trades averaged a loss of about thirteen dollars each.

The ninety one missed trades would have averaged a gain of about one thousand four hundred and seventy four dollars.

That makes sense. The trades that never came back to your price were the ones that ran away in your favour.

A limit order quietly keeps the losers and misses the best winners.

Ninety one trades out of almost six thousand sounds tiny. In money, they decided the year.

It gets worse. Price touching your limit does not mean you were filled. There may be a line of orders ahead of you at that price.

The safe test is to count a fill only when price trades at least one tick through your level.

With that rule, one hundred and fifty trades were missed, and they would have averaged about one thousand five hundred and sixty dollars.

The filled trades now lost about thirty one dollars each, about one hundred and seventy six thousand dollars for the year.

So the limit version was not a fix. It was a different way to pay.

If your platform fills limit orders on a touch, its results for limit strategies are probably too kind.

The last trap is the biggest. In my gold runs video, I showed how reading inside a candle fooled one of my tests.

Here it is on a limit scalp: buy a pullback with a limit order, take a small target or a small stop.

If the backtest lets the target count inside the same candle that filled the entry, it shows about one hundred and nine dollars per trade.

But on a thirty minute candle you cannot know if price hit the target before or after your fill.

Count the target only when that candle closes beyond it, and the same rule loses about one hundred and thirteen dollars per trade.

No slippage was changed there. Only an assumption about the order of prices inside one candle.

That is a swing of about two hundred and twenty dollars per trade, from a single line of code.

And one tick per fill is often generous.

At the open, during news releases, in thin overnight hours, and when a stop order fires inside a fast candle, fills can be several ticks away.

I did not have real fill records for this year, so I am not putting a number on that.

But a test that already breaks at one tick has no chance in those moments.

That is why I treat one tick as the minimum cost, never the expected one.

So this is the checklist I use now.

Every test pays commission and at least one tick on every fill.

I find the break-even tick. If a rule cannot survive two ticks, I do not trust it.

Limit orders only fill when price trades through my level, never on a touch.

When I cannot see inside a candle, I assume the order that hurts me.

And I check both halves of the data, because one wild month can carry a whole year.

None of these steps makes a rule better. They only stop a weak one from looking strong.
