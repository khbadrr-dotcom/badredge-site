Here is what happened.

In the copied pack, all five accounts were lost together in about forty two percent of runs.

In the spread pack, losing all five happened in about one run in a hundred.

And look at the other end. In the copied pack, all five survived in about fifty eight percent of runs. In the spread pack, only about seven percent of runs kept every account alive.

So copying does not make you lose more accounts on average. It changes how you lose them. Spread accounts fail one at a time. Copied accounts fail all at once, or not at all.

For a pack, there is almost no middle. You either keep the whole pack, or you start again from zero.

Now the part that surprised me.

The average number of accounts lost was almost the same. About two out of five, in both versions.

But the pack as a whole did better when it was spread. After sixty days, the spread pack was ahead in about sixty nine percent of runs. The copied pack, about fifty percent.

The typical result was about three thousand four hundred dollars ahead for the spread pack, and about three hundred for the copied one.

Why? Because a spread pack lets the surviving accounts keep earning after the others fall. A copied pack has nobody left to earn when it falls.

So copying is not a way to spread risk. It is a way to multiply one idea. If the idea is good and the size is right, that is powerful. If not, it is five times the damage, on one day.

There is another risk that has nothing to do with the market. The copier itself.

A follower account can miss an order, fill at a different price, or stay open after the lead has closed. One slow connection is enough.

When that happens, your five identical accounts stop being identical. One is suddenly further from its line than the others, and you might not notice until the close.

And the rules matter here too. Copying between your own accounts is often allowed. But many firms cap how many funded accounts one person can hold. Holding opposite positions across accounts is commonly banned. And copying someone else's trades, or sharing accounts, usually ends all of them.

A simple habit helps. After every trade, look at all five positions, not just the lead. If one account is still open when the others are flat, close it by hand before doing anything else.

And if a copier fails twice in a week, trade one account until you know why. A broken copier turns one plan into five different trades, and nobody planned those.

None of this is the same at every firm. Read the copy trading section of your own rules before you connect anything.
