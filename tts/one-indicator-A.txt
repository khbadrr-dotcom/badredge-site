Somewhere on your chart right now, there is an indicator you trust. A line, an arrow, a coloured dot that tells you when to act.

Someone wrote that indicator. One person, with one opinion about the market, and their own choices about every setting.

Some indicators even redraw their own past, so they look perfect when you scroll back and late when you trade live.

I have tried a lot of indicators over the years. Trying them is part of the process, and worth doing. But I never let one make the decision.

So today I put that belief to the test. One year of gold and Nasdaq, five of the most common indicators, and a few questions about how they behave.

Here is the way I think about indicators. Each one is a lens.

A moving average shows you trend. An oscillator shows you how stretched price is. A volatility band shows you how wide the day has been.

None of them adds information that is not already in the price. They take the same candles and point your eye at one part of them.

That is useful. A good lens helps you see something you would have missed.

But a lens is not a decision. Change the lens, and the same chart tells a different story. So the real question is, how often do the lenses tell the same story?

I took five very common ones. A twenty period moving average. The MACD. The RSI. Supertrend. And a simple range middle line.

Each one gets a vote on every thirty minute candle for a full year. Up or down.

On gold, all five agreed on about fifty one percent of candles. On Nasdaq, about fifty one and a half.

So about half the time, the five gave a clear, unanimous answer. And about one candle in seven, they split three to two.

That means if you pick one indicator at random, on many candles it will tell you something the others do not.

This is the first reason not to trust one. The answer often depends on which one you happened to open.

And the more indicators you stack, the more often at least one of them disagrees. More lines on a chart do not mean more certainty.

Fine, you might say. Then only trade when all five agree.

So I checked that too. On every gold candle where all five said up, was price higher two hours later?

About forty eight percent of the time. When all five said down, price was lower two hours later about half the time.

On Nasdaq, all five up was followed by a higher price about fifty three percent of the time. All five down, about forty seven.

In other words, agreement on its own was close to a coin flip over the next two hours.

That does not mean indicators are useless. It means agreement tells you what already happened. It does not, by itself, tell you what happens next.

An indicator describes the past candles. Your trade lives in the next ones.

Here is the part most traders never check. Two indicators can share a name and still disagree.

The RSI is a good example. The classic version uses a special kind of smoothing. Many coders use a plain average instead, and still call it RSI fourteen.

I ran both on the same gold candles. Typically, they were about six points apart. At times, almost forty points apart.

Now look at the famous seventy line. The plain average version was above seventy on about sixteen hundred candles. The classic version, only about seven hundred and fifty.

Twice as many "overbought" warnings, from an indicator with the same name and the same setting.

On Nasdaq it was the same story. About nineteen hundred candles against about eight hundred and fifty.

So when someone tells you the RSI is above seventy, the honest question is, whose RSI?

Even the same code gives different answers with a different setting.

On gold, an RSI with a length of seven crossed below thirty about four hundred and sixty five times in the year.

With fourteen, about a hundred and eighty times.

With twenty one, about ninety.

Same market, same year, same idea. Five times more signals at one setting than another.

So a signal is not a fact about the market. It is a fact about the market and a setting someone chose.

Moving average crossovers have the same problem.

On gold, a nine and twenty one crossover flipped about five hundred times in a year. A twenty and fifty crossover, about two hundred and twenty.

And for about one candle in five, the two pairs pointed in opposite directions. One said up, the other said down.

Neither of them was wrong. They are answering different questions. One asks about the last few hours, the other about the last day or two.

The mistake is reading one as the market's answer, when it is only the answer to the question you set it.

There is one more way the same indicator can argue with itself. The timeframe.

I put the same twenty period moving average on thirty minute candles, and on four hour candles, and asked a simple question. Is price above it or below it?

On gold, the two answers disagreed on about one candle in three. On Nasdaq, the same, about thirty four percent.

So a trader on the thirty minute chart sees a downtrend, while a trader on the four hour chart sees an uptrend. Same market, same minute, same indicator.

Both are right about their own chart. This is why many traders look at a higher timeframe first, and only then zoom in.

But it also means that a screenshot of one indicator on one timeframe is never the whole picture. Always ask which chart it came from.

Now the most dangerous kind. Indicators that repaint.

A repainting indicator changes signals that were already on the chart. An arrow appears, then moves, or disappears, after the candles have closed.

Some do it by accident, through a coding mistake that peeks at data from later candles. Some do it by design, because the method only knows the answer later.

Either way, the history you see when you scroll back is not the history you would have seen live.

And that is exactly how a weak tool can look brilliant in a screenshot.

Here is a clean example, and one you can test yourself. A swing high marker.

A common version draws a marker on a candle that is higher than the five candles before it and the five after it.

It is drawn on the top candle. But it cannot know it is the top until five more candles have closed. On thirty minute candles, that is two and a half hours later.

I counted every one on gold for the year. About seven hundred.

By the time each marker could actually appear, price had typically already fallen about two hundred and thirteen dollars from the top, on one micro gold.

More than half the time, it was already more than two hundred dollars below.

On Nasdaq, the typical drop before the marker appeared was about a hundred and ninety dollars on one micro.

So when you scroll back, every marker sits exactly on a top. It looks like the indicator called every turn.

Live, it arrived two and a half hours later, after a big part of the move was gone.

This is not a scam. It is a normal tool used the wrong way. The problem starts when someone sells the rear-view mirror as a windscreen.

There is a simple test. Put the indicator on a chart, and watch it live, candle by candle, for a few days. Take a screenshot each hour.

If an old signal moves or vanishes, you have your answer. Some platforms also have a replay mode that shows the same thing faster.

And be careful with backtests built on these tools. A test that uses the marker where it is drawn, instead of where it appeared, is testing the rear-view mirror. The results will look far better than anything you could have traded.

So how do I actually use indicators? Like a pilot uses gauges.

I check a few, each for one job. One for trend, one for how stretched price is, one for where the key levels are.

But the decision comes from a plan written before the session. The level, the risk, the size, and what would prove me wrong.

The indicators help me see the plan. They do not write it.

On a normal morning, that means maybe three things on my chart, not fifteen. Every extra line is one more voice that can talk me out of my plan, or into a trade I never planned.

And when two of my gauges disagree, that is information too. It usually means the market has not made up its mind, and neither should I.

Before you trust any indicator, put it through five questions.

One. Does it repaint? Watch it live before you believe its history.

Two. What exactly does it calculate? If the code is closed, you are trusting someone else's math.

Three. How sensitive is it to its settings? Move each setting a little. If the signals change a lot, the tool is fragile.

Four. Was it tested on data it was not built on? A tool tuned on last year can look perfect on last year.

Five. What would you do without it? If the answer is nothing, the indicator is making your decision.

A few honest limits.

This is one year of gold and Nasdaq, on thirty minute candles. Other markets, timeframes and years will give other numbers.

I tested five common indicators with their usual settings. There are thousands of others, and some are built for other jobs.

And the two hour check is one simple question. It is not a full test of any strategy.

So, never trust one indicator. Use several as lenses, test the ones you rely on, and keep the decision in your own plan.
