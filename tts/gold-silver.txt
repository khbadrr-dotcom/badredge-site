On the second of March, gold closed at a new twenty day high. Silver did not.

Many traders read that as a warning. If silver will not follow, the rally is running out of fuel.

This time the warning looked brilliant. Ten trading days later, gold was about three hundred dollars lower.

But one chart proves nothing. So I counted every time this happened since early 2016, almost eleven years of daily closes.

And the answer is not the one the chart above suggests.

The idea is old. The two metals usually move together, because the same things push them: the dollar, interest rates, fear.

So when both make new highs, people call it confirmation. Everyone agrees.

When only gold makes the new high, people call it a disagreement. Something is off, and the move is suspect.

It sounds sensible. The question is whether it ever mattered for what gold did next.

Here is the rule, fixed before I looked at any result.

A disagreement is a day when gold closes above every close of the previous twenty trading days, and silver does not.

An agreement is a day when both do it together.

If the same thing happened again within five days, I counted it once, so one long rally does not get counted ten times.

Then I measured where gold closed five, ten and twenty trading days later. Ten days is the main number in this video.

And I tested a simple trade. Short gold at the next open after a disagreement, and close it ten trading days later, after fees.

Since early 2016, gold made eighty of these disagreements.

And eighty three agreements, where silver joined in.

So it is about an even split. When gold makes a fresh high, silver joins it about half the time.

That alone tells you something. A disagreement is not rare. It shows up about seven or eight times a year. If it were a strong warning, gold would have struggled to climb.

Over the same years, gold went from about eleven hundred dollars to over five thousand.

So what did gold do ten trading days after silver refused to follow?

It was higher about sixty one percent of the time.

On any random day in the same years, gold was higher ten days later about fifty six percent of the time.

And after the agreements, the days that were supposed to be the healthy ones, about fifty three.

Read that again. The warning was followed by gold going up slightly more often than normal, not less.

The average move tells a similar story. About half a percent up, whether silver agreed or not.

Maybe ten days is too long, or too short. So I looked at five days and twenty days as well, on the years before this last one.

After five days, gold was higher about fifty nine percent of the time after a disagreement. After twenty days, about sixty one.

On ordinary days, about fifty seven and fifty eight.

And after agreements, about fifty one, and then below half at twenty days.

So at every horizon, the picture was the same. The disagreement did not come before weakness. If anything, the agreements did, slightly. That is the opposite of the classic reading, though the gaps are small enough that I would not build anything on them.

Now the trade that a believer would take. Short gold at the next open, hold for ten trading days.

It won forty percent of the time.

After costs, it lost about three hundred and seventy dollars per contract per trade on average.

Over all eighty trades, about thirty thousand dollars lost on one contract.

And shorting after the agreements was worse, about eighteen hundred dollars lost per trade. That makes sense. Shorting gold during a decade when it more than tripled was a hard game either way.

But the point here is simple. Treating the disagreement as a sell warning did not pay.

Was it the same every year? No.

In 2016 to 2019, gold was higher after most disagreements. Twenty one out of twenty seven.

From 2020 to 2022, it flipped. Eight out of twenty one. In 2022, only two out of eight.

Then in 2024 and 2025, gold rose after most of them again.

So in some years the warning looked right, in others it looked useless. That pattern matches gold's own trend in those years more than it matches silver.

Which suggests the disagreement was mostly telling you what gold was already doing.

Back to the second of March.

That day, gold closed near five thousand three hundred and twelve, above its old high of about five thousand two hundred and forty eight.

Silver closed near eighty eight dollars eighty five, still well below its high of about ninety three from the twenty seventh of February.

Ten trading days later, gold was near five thousand and two. About three hundred and nine dollars lower.

This is the chart people share. And it is real. It just is not the typical case.

Now the eleventh of August. That day, gold closed at a new twenty day high, near four thousand four hundred and forty one.

Silver closed near sixty four dollars ninety four. Its high was about sixty five twenty seven, set the day before.

Silver missed by about thirty four cents. By the rule, that is a disagreement.

Ten days later, gold was about two hundred and fifty three dollars higher.

So a "disagreement" can be a few cents. That made me ask a fair question. What if silver has to lag clearly?

What about this last year on its own? Gold had a wild year, up from about four thousand to over five thousand three hundred, and back to about three thousand five hundred.

There were only five disagreements. Ten days later, gold was higher after three of them.

And five agreements, with gold higher after four.

Over the same year, on any random day, gold was higher ten days later only about forty four percent of the time, because the year ended lower.

Five cases each is far too few to say anything. I show it only so you know the recent year does not hide a different answer.

So I tightened it. Silver had to be at least two percent below its own high. This rule came after the first results, so treat it as a check.

Thirty one cases. Ten days later, gold was higher about fifty eight percent of the time, and the average move was about zero.

The fade trade looked better. It averaged about sixteen hundred dollars a trade.

But look at how. It won only forty five percent of the time, and the typical trade lost about sixty five dollars.

Two trades did almost all the work. The twentieth of October 2025, and the second of March 2026. Together, about sixty nine thousand dollars.

Take out those two, and the same rule lost about eighteen thousand.

Found after looking. A result that rests on two trades needs forward testing, not trust.

The same idea works in reverse. The yellow metal makes a new twenty day low, and silver does not.

That happened fifty nine times.

Ten days later, gold was higher about sixty eight percent of the time, against about fifty six on any day.

That is the biggest gap in this whole study. But I did not go in looking for it, and fifty nine cases in a decade that mostly went up is a thin base.

Found after looking. Needs forward testing.

Maybe twenty days is the wrong window. So I tried ten days and fifty days.

With ten days, a hundred and eight cases, and gold was higher about fifty nine percent of the time afterwards.

With fifty days, fifty seven cases, about sixty three percent.

The fade trade lost money on all three windows.

So the result is not a quirk of one setting. Whichever window you pick, the disagreement did not mark tops more often than chance.

So why does the idea feel so convincing?

Because when it works, it works loudly. The twentieth of October 2025 was one of those. That day, gold closed near four thousand three hundred and fifty nine, silver lagged, and two weeks later gold was about three hundred and forty five dollars lower.

Charts like this get saved and shared. The many quiet cases where gold just kept climbing do not.

There is also a simple reason it should be weak. Silver is more jumpy than gold. It misses new highs all the time for reasons that have nothing to do with gold.

And a few cents can turn an agreement into a disagreement, as August showed.

So what do I take from this?

First, a gold high without silver is not a sell signal on its own. Across almost eleven years, it was followed by gold rising slightly more often than usual.

Second, if you watch silver, look at how far it lags, not just whether it lags. A few cents means very little.

Third, when a rule looks good, count the trades that carry it. If two trades make the whole result, you have a story, not an edge.

And if you trade with this idea, log your own cases. Date, the gap in silver, and what gold did ten days later. Your own fifty will teach you more than any chart.

A few honest limits.

This uses daily closes only. Intraday disagreements, on an hourly chart for example, could behave differently.

The prices are continuous front month futures. Contract rolls can shift a close slightly.

The last year alone had only five disagreements, too few to say anything about this year by itself.

And the clear lag rule, the low side and the window checks were all found after looking. None of them is ready to trade until it is tested forward.

So, when gold and silver disagree, does it matter? In almost eleven years of data, not in the way most people think. Prices rose slightly more often afterwards, and fading it lost money.
