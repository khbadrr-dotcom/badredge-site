Open almost any trading video about gold, and you will see one line drawn through the chart. VWAP.

People say it is where the big money trades. That price respects it. That it acts like a magnet in the morning and a wall in the afternoon.

It is one of the most popular indicators on YouTube. Strategy videos about it reach hundreds of thousands of views.

But almost nobody shows you the count. How often does gold actually turn at VWAP, across a whole year, every touch included?

So I counted. Two hundred and fifty seven trading days of gold futures, with real volume, and one rule fixed before I looked.

First, what the line actually is, in one sentence.

VWAP is the average price of the day, where every candle counts by how much was traded in it.

A busy candle with a lot of volume pulls the line hard. A quiet candle barely moves it.

It starts fresh every day. For gold futures, that is six in the evening, New York time.

So by the afternoon, VWAP is roughly the average price that everyone who traded that day paid. That is why people think it matters. If price is above it, the average buyer of the day is in profit.

Here is the rule, in plain words.

A touch is a thirty minute candle that reaches the line after price has closed on one side of it twice in a row.

A bounce means price then moves one average candle away from the line, in the direction it came from, within four hours.

A cut through means a candle closes half an average candle on the other side first.

I skipped the first hour of each day, because the line is too new to mean anything there.

And I tested two other lines with the exact same rule, so the result has something to be compared with. I will show you those in a moment.

In one year, gold touched VWAP two thousand two hundred and seventy five times.

It touched it on two hundred and fifty five of the two hundred and fifty seven days. So almost every single day.

About five percent of touches did neither, no bounce and no cut within four hours. I left those out of the percentages.

That leaves about two thousand one hundred and seventy touches with a clear ending. A big enough sample to trust the main number.

And to keep it fair, a new touch only counted after price had closed twice on one side again. So one long chop around the line does not count as twenty touches.

Here is one more number that explains a lot.

On a typical day, a thirty minute close crossed from one side of VWAP to the other about six times.

A line that price crosses six times a day is not acting like a wall. That is the plainest way to read this whole study.

So what is VWAP good for?

As a reference, it is honest. It tells you where the average trader of the day stands, and whether price is above or below that.

As a reason to enter on its own, this year of gold says no. A touch was a door that swings both ways.

If you want to use it, the data points to two habits. Pay the most attention to the first touch of the day. And be very careful with touches against the slope of the line.

And if you use it with other tools, test the combination yourself. The indicator video explains why stacking lines that measure the same thing does not add much.

Keep a simple log. The touch, first or later, the slope, bounce or cut, and the result after costs. Fifty of your own will tell you more than any video.

A few honest limits.

This is one written rule. A tighter or looser bounce test could give different numbers.

It is one year of gold futures on thirty minute candles. Inside a candle, I cannot see whether the high or the low came first, so the stop always counted first.

Anchored VWAP, weekly VWAP and the bands around it are different tools. I did not test them here.

And every split, first touch, slope and session, was found after looking. None of it is ready to trade until it is tested forward.

So, does gold respect VWAP? About as much as it respects a line I made up. Half the touches bounced, half cut through.

The first touch of the day is the one place that stood out. And it is worth testing yourself.
